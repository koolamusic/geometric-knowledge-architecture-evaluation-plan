# Amendments

## Version 2 (tag plan-v2), 2026-10-15

Made before any evaluation data existed. Every change adds a baseline, test, margin or sample, corrects arithmetic, or clarifies wording; no success bar was lowered, no sample size reduced, and no stopping or drop rule loosened. One secondary metric (MRR) moved to exploratory. In the paper, the introduction to Section 10 now carries one sentence pointing to this registration.

Each item is what changed in Section 10, before → after, and why. No threshold was lowered, no sample was shrunk, no stopping rule was loosened and no drop condition was softened.

1. Registration. Before: the bars, bounds, sample sizes, stopping rules and drop rules "are fixed by this paper". After: they "were registered publicly before any data existed, do not change once held-out data has been scored, and any deviation is reported against that registration (Miracle, 2026)". Why: the plan is now fixed outside the paper.
2. Matched budgets. Before: "Quality is compared at these matched budgets". After: quality is compared "at matched tuning budgets, not matched spend"; the system's write spend, which no baseline incurs, is charged in the 10.4 ledger. Why: says plainly what is and is not matched.
3. Design effect. Before: "the smallest difference worth acting on". After: "the true difference the test is powered to detect". Why: an observed gain below the design effect can still be significant, so the old wording overstated what the bar guarantees. Raising the bar to require an observed gain at the design effect would halve power, so the bar is unchanged and every gain is now reported with its confidence interval.
4. Implied bar. Before: at least about 0.66 of the design effect at the planned size and 0.47 at the cap, computed at one-sided 0.05. After: about 0.76 and 0.53, computed at the nominal one-sided 0.03 actually used at each look. Why: the figures now match the level the test uses.
5. Preference primary in 10.2. Before: the sizing text referred to "the primary's 0.03 per look" while single-proportion bars were "one look, and no extension", so whether the extension applied was unclear. After: the preference primary "is tested once, at one-sided 0.03, with no extension". Why: removes the ambiguity in the stricter direction; 225 decided pairs, sized at 0.05/3, cover it.
6. Ties and non-inferiority. Before: the non-inferiority exceptions named only 10.5 and the 10.2 fidelity guard. After: they name 10.1 (new item 8) as well. In 10.5, "This is the plan's one non-inferiority test" becomes "It is a non-inferiority test because …", which is wording only; no threshold, sample or rule changed. Why: consistency with item 8.
7. 10.1 secondary metric. Before: "Recall@50 and MRR". After: Recall@50 secondary; MRR "reported as exploratory only, since reciprocal rank is not interval-scaled and is not averaged into any decision". Why: reciprocal rank is not interval-scaled, so its mean should not decide anything.
8. 10.1 per-workspace clause. Before: "no workspace shows a significant degradation", a clause that passes on non-significance in unpowered per-workspace tests. After: in every workspace the lower confidence bound on the Dimension gain at its Holm level lies above −0.03; with about 90 held-out queries per workspace, all three pass with about 80% probability when the true gain is 0.03. Why: a pass now requires positive evidence against a degradation larger than the design effect. Every outcome that failed the old clause fails the new one.
9. 10.1 HyDE escalation. Before: tested on the queries where it fires, with "the firing count and the effect it can detect at 80% power reported before scoring". After: design effect 0.05 nDCG@10, for which about 56 firing queries give 80% power; escalation is kept only if at least that many fire and the difference is positive and significant, and is removed otherwise. Why: the detectable effect is fixed in advance, and an underpowered test cannot keep the escalation.
10. 10.2 baseline (b). Before: "an entity graph with extracted entity claims and community summaries". After: adds that claim extraction is "a step Edge et al. (2024) describe but did not evaluate". Why: says plainly what the baseline relies on.
11. 10.2 small-tier deriver. Before: no arm tested derivation on a small model. After: "An exploratory arm (d) runs the full system with derivation on the small tier", as a check on the argument that derivation needs the strongest model. Why: puts that argument under observation.
12. 10.3 automatic acceptance baseline. Before: 150 automatically accepted Claims judged against an absolute bar only. After: 230 accepted Claims and, as baseline, 230 drawn at random from all Claims the slow path wrote, which is what accepting everything would accept. Annotators are blind to sample. The rule must pass the absolute bar and also exceed the baseline's precision by a one-sided two-proportion test at 0.05, design effect 0.10 (230 per sample give 80% power at 0.8 against 0.7). The samples overlap, which makes the comparison conservative. If the rule fails either test it is disabled. Why: the rule now has to beat accepting everything, not only clear a fixed bar. The sample was raised to power the comparison.
13. 10.4 Graphiti-style comparison. Before: if that arm matched the system's staleness at no higher ingest cost, "the justification network is not earning its complexity on this axis", with no consequence. After: "matches" means the system's probe staleness is not significantly below the arm's, by a one-sided two-proportion test at 0.05 on the pooled final-checkpoint probes (about 110 per arm give 80% power at 0.05 against 0.15). The consequence is binding: the Signal branch of the contradiction check and lifting are removed. Why: the comparison now has a defined test and a binding outcome.
14. 10.4 parity rule. Before: parity is "not significantly better", with no stated rationale. After: the same rule, stated to be deliberate, being "the stricter rule against the system". Why: adding an equivalence margin would let the system set a more expensive read baseline, which would loosen the cost test.
15. 10.4 human curation. Before: Domain creation was "reported as its own line and charged to neither side". After: it is still reported as its own line, and is now charged to the system, with Domain creation listed among the curation actions. The counts include "the Domain creations and revisions in the log window". Why: no curation cost is left out of the break-even figure.
16. 10.4 price-ratio sensitivity. Before: a change in the frontier-to-small price ratio left N*/m "roughly fixed" with the reasoning arm at parity. After: the ratio moves W, g_base and r_base but not H, the ingest line or r_fast, so N*/m is not fixed. A higher ratio lowers it, a lower one can remove break-even, as the illustration shows, and with plain retrieval at parity N*/m rises with the ratio. Why: the old statement contradicted the plan's own illustration.
17. 10.4 illustration. Before: "a fast-path read costs 30 small calls". After: "the equivalent of 30 small calls, mostly cross-encoder scoring with query projection within the 10% bound of Section 10.1". Why: consistency with the 10.1 cost bound. No number changed.
18. 10.4 cost-half test sizing. Before: the bootstrap upper bound on N* had no sample size, power or minimum detectable margin. After: the test is sized before the replay, by simulation from internal-pilot costs and the pilot-log N̄, for 80% power at a minimum detectable margin of a true N* at half of N̄·m. Reads and costed slow-path requests are increased up to three times the planned counts. The extra reads and runs are costed only, never judged, so the quality samples and their cap of twice the planned size are unchanged. The extra runs come from the same log window and also fill the acceptance samples of 10.3. Sizes are reported before scoring, and a shortfall at the cap moves the margin under the shortfall rule. Why: the cost-half test is now powered like every other confirmatory clause. Sample sizes can only rise.
